1 · How well did the agents perform on each ticket?

#101 Bulldog tee — strong (the hardest ticket, handled well)

This was the only ticket with a real dependency chain, and the team got it right. Inventory read CC-TEE-WHITE/S = 0 against 1 requested, matched vendor 1 (apparel reprint, 5-day lead), and — crucially — called vendor_has_open_invoice(1) and found the vendor blocked by unpaid invoice #501. It handed off to accounting, which prepared the $840 payment that unblocks the vendor and the reprint PO (see the #101 Actual tool list). The Cash tab shows the exact right consequence: −$840, and the PO was not charged because the vendor hasn't invoiced the reprint yet. The one real miss: no customer reply was drafted to Tauhid, so operationally the shop knows what to do but the customer hasn't been told. Grade: high, docked only for the missing communication.

#102 Rent due — excellent (clean and minimal)

Textbook. Facilities verified lease 1 ($2,400, due 2026-09-02 — two days after the shop's today of 2026-08-31), accounting confirmed it was affordable and prepared the payment, the human approved it: Cash tab shows −$2,400 → $160. Three agents, each doing exactly one necessary thing, nothing wasted, no customer or stock noise. Best-performing ticket.

#103 Bulk hoodie discount — mixed (correct but thin)

Accounting's analysis was accurate — list $58, cost $22, $36 margin (62.1%) — and it correctly noted the ticket gives no discount amount and there is no price-change tool, so it stopped at a recommendation (Cash: $0). That is a defensible, honest call. But for a real shop it under-delivered: it never pulled inventory to flag that M is 8 on hand vs 20 requested (a 12-unit shortfall that would need a restock), and it drafted no quote back to Yale AI Club. The margin math is right; the ticket is only half-served. Weakest performance.

3 · What would have been simpler as one agent with tools?

All three of these tickets would have been simpler — and cheaper — as a single "ops" agent holding the full MCP toolset. The evidence is in the shapes on the ticket tabs: every ticket is a linear, 1–2 step job, not a problem that needs parallel or specialized reasoning.

  • #102 is the clearest case. The entire job is get_lease → get_cash_balance → prepare_payment. One agent does that in a few model calls. Instead, boss→facilities→accounting meant three separate agent runs (three system prompts, three message histories) for a two-tool task — the delegation tripled the token cost without changing the answer.
  • #103 was literally one agent's worth of work in the end (get_pricing + a judgement call), yet still paid for a boss→accounting hop.
  • #101 has the most steps, but they're still sequential and dependent (check stock → find vendor → see block → pay → order). A single agent with the same tools walks that chain without any hand-off overhead.

Why one agent would be better here: the five-agent split adds a model round-trip per hand-off and a chance for the boss to stop early (which is exactly how #101's draft and #103's restock got dropped). Multi-agent structure earns its cost when sub-tasks are genuinely parallel, need different tools or permissions, or need isolation for audit. For a 3-ticket board of short linear tasks, one well-prompted agent with the whole toolbox (and the same human-approval guardrails) is simpler, cheaper, and would have been more complete. The team design pays off at larger scale, not on this board.

4 · Three new problems this team could solve today

These need only the tools already built (reads, prepare_*, pay, the approval UI).

  1. A second vendor invoice comes due. If Elm City Gifts (vendor 2) sends an invoice, accounting can list_invoices, compare the due date to get_today, and prepare_payment; the human approves and pay enforces the balance. Same machinery as invoice #501 on #101.
  2. Another out-of-stock customer order, e.g. the Crest Mug (CC-MUG-CREST/OS = 0). Inventory's check_stock finds the zero, list_vendors picks Elm City Gifts (mugs, 3-day lead), vendor_has_open_invoice checks the block, accounting prepares the restock PO — identical pattern to the tee on #101.
  3. A payment the shop can't afford. With cash now at $160, any new rent/invoice above that is caught: prepare_payment returns affordable=false and pay refuses rather than going negative (the Cash tab shows how tight the balance already is). The team can correctly report "cannot cover — needs cash" instead of overdrawing.

5 · Three new problems this team cannot solve — and what they'd need

  1. Recording money coming in (a completed sale). The shop is cash-out only — there is no deposit/revenue tool and no orders table, so the team can't record the Yale AI Club actually buying the hoodies, or reconcile income against the rent it just paid. Needs: a record_sale / deposit tool (and the cash-only-out rule relaxed to a real ledger), an orders table, and arguably a Sales agent to own fulfilment and revenue.
  2. Actually contacting the customer or vendor. Everything external is a draft or a prepared record — save_customer_message never sends, and a PO is never transmitted to the vendor. The shop can't tell Tauhid his tee is coming or place the reprint order for real. Needs: a gated send_message / submit_purchase_order integration tool (email / vendor API) behind the same human-approval step, so drafts become real communications without losing the guardrail.
  3. Enacting a price change or receiving a shipment. #103 could only recommend a discount because there is no pricing-write tool, and even an approved restock PO can never update stock because nothing increments inventory.qty when goods arrive. Needs: an apply_discount / set_price tool (with a promotions table) and a receive_shipment tool that adds the delivered units after the lead time — plus a Merchandising agent to own pricing and a receiving step to close the restock loop. Without these, the team can plan money and stock but can't change prices or actually replenish shelves.
